Reading cracks in your walls

Almost every house has cracks. Here is how to tell the harmless ones from the ones worth acting on, using what you can see and measure yourself.

Plaster shrinks, timber moves and paint dries out, so hairline cracks appear in most homes sooner or later. The cracks that matter are the ones that grow, that go right through a wall, or that come with other signs such as doors that stop closing. This page helps you sort one from the other. It is general information, and only a proper inspection of your own house can say what is happening.

The four things to look at

  1. Width.Hairline cracks under 1mm are usually cosmetic. Between roughly 1mm and 5mm, a crack deserves watching. Past about 5mm it is time to have someone qualified look at it. A £1 coin is about 3mm thick, which is a handy rule of thumb.
  2. Direction.Diagonal cracks running up from the corner of a door or window, or stepping through brickwork along the mortar joints, are more often linked to movement in the ground than straight vertical or horizontal ones.
  3. Taper.A crack that is wider at the top than the bottom, or the reverse, suggests part of the building has dropped. A crack of even width along its length is more often shrinkage or thermal movement.
  4. Both sides.Check inside and outside. A crack you can see on both faces of the same wall goes right through it, and that is more significant than a surface crack in plaster.

A rough guide to crack widths

Building researchers group crack damage into categories by width, and surveyors often quote them. The figures below are a guide, not a diagnosis.

Crack widthWhat it usually means
Under 0.1mm (hairline)Negligible. Normal drying and settling.
Up to 1mmVery slight. Fill and redecorate; keep an eye on it.
1mm to 5mmSlight to moderate. Worth monitoring, especially if it is diagonal or growing.
5mm to 15mmModerate to severe. Have it looked at. Doors and windows may stick.
15mm and aboveSevere. Get advice promptly; there may be a safety issue.

Signs that point towards subsidence

  • Cracks that are wider than 3mm or are getting wider
  • Diagonal or stepped cracks near door and window corners, or above lintels
  • Cracks that appear within a few months and are wider at one end
  • Cracks that open up in late summer and autumn and partly close in winter
  • Doors and windows that stick, or gaps opening between a wall and the ceiling or floor
  • Cracks in the same area of the house as a large tree, a leaking drain or a recent extension

Signs it may be something else

  • Hairline cracks that follow plasterboard joints or the corner between wall and ceiling
  • Cracks in new plaster or fresh paint within the first year or two
  • Vertical cracks where an extension meets the main house, which may be separate movement; see Extensions, Bays and Garden Walls
  • Horizontal cracks along a line of mortar in an outside wall, which can point to wall-tie corrosion rather than ground movement

What to do now

  1. Photograph it with a ruler beside it,and note the date. Take a wider shot to show where it sits on the wall.
  2. Measure and recordthe width at its widest point. Repeat every couple of weeks. A pencil mark at each end of the crack with a date also works.
  3. Do not fill it yet.Filling hides the evidence and makes it harder to tell whether it is still moving.
  4. Look for causes: trees and large shrubs close to the wall, damp patches outside, gurgling drains, and whether a neighbour has done building work.
  5. If it looks significant,read about claiming on buildings insurance and send an enquiry so a specialist firm can take a look.

If you see a crack that is rapidly growing, bulging brickwork, or a wall that is visibly leaning, treat it as urgent. Keep people out of the area and ask a structural engineer or your insurer for advice straight away.

Frequently asked questions

What size crack is a worry?

Cracks over about 3mm, or ones that are growing, are worth getting looked at. Hairline cracks under 1mm are normally cosmetic.

Is a diagonal crack always subsidence?

No. Diagonal cracks near doors and windows are a common sign of ground movement, but they can also be caused by other things, including lintel problems or thermal movement. It takes an inspection to tell.

Should I fill the crack?

Leave it unfilled until you know whether it is moving. Filling hides the evidence that a surveyor or insurer will want to measure.

Do cracks that close up in winter mean the problem has gone?

Not necessarily. With clay soils, cracks can open in dry months and partly close when the ground is wet again. Seasonal movement is a classic pattern, so it is worth recording through the year.
