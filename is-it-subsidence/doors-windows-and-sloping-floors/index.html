Doors, windows and sloping floors

Sometimes the first sign of movement is not a crack at all. A door that rubs, a window that will not shut or a floor that feels off can say a lot.

When part of a house moves, the frame around every door and window shifts a little with it. Because doors and windows are made to fit tightly, even a few millimetres of movement can show up as something that suddenly sticks, rattles or will not latch. Floors can slope for the same reason. None of these on their own prove subsidence, but together with cracks they are a useful clue.

Doors and windows

  • A door that used to close and now rubs at the top corner can mean the frame has dropped or twisted.
  • Gaps that are wider on one side of a frame than the other suggest racking, where a rectangle has been pushed into a parallelogram.
  • Windows that jam in one season and free up in another can point to seasonal movement. Wood also swells with damp, so check that first.
  • Cracks starting at the corners of door and window openings, usually running diagonally up or down, are a classic pattern. See Reading Cracks in Your Walls.

Sloping or bouncy floors

Ground-floor floors that slope towards one corner or edge can point to foundation movement, but they can also be caused by old timber problems or past alterations. A few things to look at:

  • Is the slope worse near an outside wall, a bay window or a part of the house built at a different time?
  • Are skirting boards pulling away from the floor, or is there a visible gap between skirting and wall?
  • Does the floor move or feel springy underfoot? That tends to mean a timber or damp problem rather than ground movement.
  • Is the slope something you have always had? In older Leicester houses, a bit of slope is common and may be centuries-old movement that finished long ago.

A simple check you can do

Put a marble or a round pencil on a hard floor and see which way it goes. It is crude, but a consistent roll towards the same side of the house over several days is a useful thing to tell a surveyor. A spirit level and a straight board laid across the floor will give a better picture, and a phone photo with the level in shot is good evidence.

Other explanations to rule out

  • Damp or humidity making timber swell
  • Rotten joists or woodworm in a suspended timber floor
  • Recent building work, such as a new door lining, that has not been fitted evenly
  • Normal seasonal swelling of wooden frames

When to take it further

If doors and windows have changed within the last few months, and especially if there are also new cracks, it is worth recording what you see and talking to a specialist. Photograph the gap at the top corner of the frame with a ruler in shot, then do the same every few weeks. If you have buildings insurance, read about making a claim before you do any work.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my door suddenly sticking?

The most common reason is damp swelling the timber. If the change is sudden, the gap is uneven and there are also cracks nearby, the frame may have moved because the wall around it has.

Can a sloping floor be fixed?

It depends on the cause. If the foundations are moving, that has to be dealt with first. Once the building is stable, floors can be levelled or relaid.

Does a sloping floor mean subsidence?

Not by itself. Many older homes have always had a slope. Look for new change, cracks in the walls and skirting boards that have pulled away.
