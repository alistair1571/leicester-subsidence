Trees, roots and house movement

On clay soils, a big tree can draw so much water that the ground shrinks beneath nearby foundations. Cutting it down is not always the answer.

A large tree can take hundreds of litres of water from the soil on a hot day. Clay soil shrinks as it dries, so the ground under nearby foundations can drop in the summer and sometimes does not fully recover. Trees are the most common cause of subsidence claims on clay in Britain, and Leicester has plenty of clay. See Clay and Ground Under Leicester Houses.

Which trees cause the most trouble

Species that take up a lot of water and grow big are the main suspects: oaks, poplars, willows, elms, limes, plane trees and some ash and horse chestnut. Hedges and large shrubs can matter too. Distance guidance varies, but as a rough rule the damage risk rises when a mature tree is closer to the house than its own height, and willows and poplars can affect houses further away.

The trickier question: what to do about the tree

Removing a tree that is causing shrinkage can stop the movement, but on clay it can also cause heave. The ground takes up water again over the following years and swells, pushing the foundations up. That is why you should not fell a nearby tree on your own initiative. A tree that has been there for decades may be part of the balance the house has settled into.

Options include removing the tree, reducing its crown or root pruning, and managing the water balance more gently over a longer time. Which is right depends on the evidence. An arboricultural report usually includes root identification, tree species and soil tests. See tree work for subsidence.

Permissions: you may need consent first

  • Tree Preservation Orders (TPOs) protect individual trees or groups. Cutting or damaging a protected tree without consent can be a criminal offence.
  • Conservation areas protect trees too, and you normally have to give the council six weeks' written notice before work on most trees. Several areas of Leicester, including parts of Stoneygate, are conservation areas, so check before you start.
  • Trees that belong to someone else, such as a neighbour or a council, cannot be cut down without their agreement. You may cut back branches overhanging your land, but not roots or branches that would harm the tree's health or stability, and it is best to ask first.

If the tree is on council land, see Council Trees and Subsidence in Leicester.

How an insurer approaches it

Insurers often investigate the tree as part of a claim. They may ask for the tree's owner to be contacted, for root samples and for monitoring data, and in some cases they pursue a claim against a tree's owner. You do not have to resolve this yourself, but you will help the claim by keeping a clear record. See Claiming on Buildings Insurance.

What to do next

Photograph the tree and its distance from the house, note its species if you know it and record the cracks. Do not carry out any tree work until you have asked your insurer or a specialist.

Frequently asked questions

Should I cut down a tree near my house?

Not before getting advice. On clay, removing a tree can cause the ground to swell and push the foundations up, which is called heave. Your insurer or an arboriculturist can advise.

How close is too close?

There is no single figure. Risk rises when a mature tree is nearer than its own height and the soil is shrinkable clay, and water-hungry species can affect houses at greater distances.

Do I need permission to prune a tree?

If it has a Tree Preservation Order or is in a conservation area, you normally do. Check with the council before any work.

Can I claim against a neighbour's tree?

Your insurer may do this as part of dealing with the claim. It is worth telling them where the tree is and who owns it.
