Clay and ground under Leicester houses

Subsidence is a ground problem before it is a house problem. Here is what is under Leicester and why it matters.

Send a free enquiry How this service works

Leicester sits in the East Midlands on a mixture of mudstones, clays and river deposits. The detail varies from street to street, which is why two similar houses can behave differently. This page gives a general picture; the British Geological Survey has the detail for your own address.

The general picture

  • Much of the city is underlain by Mercia Mudstone, a red-brown rock which breaks down at the surface into clay
  • In places this is covered by glacial deposits left by ice-age ice sheets, including stiff boulder clay (till), sand and gravel
  • Along the River Soar and its tributaries there are river deposits: alluvium, which can be soft and compressible, and river terrace gravels
  • To the north-west, towards Charnwood, there are much older, harder rocks at or near the surface

This is a summary, and it will not be right for every street. Check the BGS maps for your own address (see below).

Why clay causes trouble

Clay soils swell when wet and shrink when dry. The top few metres see the biggest changes, particularly where trees and shrubs take up water. Foundations that are shallow in relation to those changes can move up and down with the seasons, and in a dry summer the drop can be enough to crack walls. The British Geological Survey rates shrink-swell hazard on its mapping, and it is one of the main sources insurers and surveyors use.

Other ground problems

  • Soft alluvium near rivers and old stream courses can compress under load
  • Made ground where land has been filled or levelled, for example old brickworks, quarries or railway cuttings, may be poorly compacted
  • Old workings are uncommon under most of the city but exist elsewhere in Leicestershire; see Mining and Ground Voids in Leicestershire

Check your own street

  1. BGS GeoIndex or the BGS map viewershows the type of rock and surface deposits. It is free to use.
  2. Look at the shrink-swell ratingif you can find it for your location. Higher ratings mean more movement risk where there are trees or drying.
  3. Check what is nearby: a mature tree, a stream, a former pit or a recent build next door all matter as much as the underlying rock.
  4. Ask a specialistif you want to know how it applies to your house; ground investigation or boreholes are the definitive answer.

What this means for your house

Houses built before modern foundation standards often sit on shallow footings, typically around a metre or less. Modern homes on clay are usually built on deeper foundations designed to cope. If you live in an older Leicester terrace, a bay window or extension on clay is a classic place for seasonal movement. See Extensions, Bays and Garden Walls and Trees, Roots and House Movement.

Frequently asked questions

Is Leicester built on clay?

Much of it is, either clay-rich mudstone or glacial deposits, although the detail varies a lot. The British Geological Survey maps show what is under your street.

Does clay always cause subsidence?

No. Problems happen when clay dries out, for example from tree roots or a hot summer, and when foundations are shallow.

Where do I find out what my ground is like?

The BGS website has free maps. A specialist or engineer can advise on what the ground means for your house.
